## Cultural notes

Besides the lessons in pronunciation, all of the dialogs key on some cultural issue that Valdo and Michelle have noticed as Brazilians living in the United States.

Brazilians are used to a system of public health that is free. They are not used to our insurance systems with primary providers, co-payments, etc. In this dialog the Brazilians are still a little confused about why it would cost so much money to get a cast, even when you already have insurance.

## Pronunciation notes

This dialog focuses on the sounds for <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">/ê/</span> (also called "closed e") and <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">/é/</span> (also called "open e"). The "closed e" is similar to the sound of English words *h<mark>ay</mark>*, *h<mark>a</mark>te*, *b<mark>ay</mark>*, *b<mark>ai</mark>t* and exactly like the vowel "e" in Spanish, as in words like <i lang="es">b<mark>e</mark>b<mark>e</mark></i>, <i lang="es">ll<mark>e</mark>v<mark>e</mark>n</i>, <i lang="es">d<mark>e</mark>sd<mark>e</mark></i>. The "open e" is similar to sounds in English words that are generally spelled with an "e", as in *l<mark class="blue">e</mark>t*, *g<mark class="blue">e</mark>t*, *b<mark class="blue">e</mark>tter*, *cr<mark class="blue">e</mark>dit*.

As a general rule of thumb, the "open e" is only found in stressed syllables in Portuguese. Of course in actual pronunciation things are more complicated, but as a general rule, if the syllable is not stressed, the "e" will be closed. If the syllable is stressed, the "e" may be either open or closed. Here are a few tendencies to keep in mind:

- Words written with a circumflex accent are closed: <i lang="pt-BR">voc<mark>ê</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">por qu<mark>ê</mark></i>
- Words written with an acute accent are open: <i lang="pt-BR">at<mark class="blue">é</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark class="blue">é</mark></i>
- Many words that have the diphthong "ie" in Spanish have an open pronunciation in Portuguese: <i lang="es">quiero</i> > <i lang="pt-BR">qu<mark class="blue">e</mark>ro</i>, <i lang="es">fiesta</i> > <i lang="pt-BR">f<mark class="blue">e</mark>sta</i>
- Nasal consonants that follow "e" produce a closed sound: <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>e</mark>nsa</i> (despite SP <i lang="es">piensa</i>)

The challenge for learners who speak English is to avoid pronouncing the "closed e" as a glide (a combination of "e" and "i"). In Portuguese the presence or absence of the glide will change the meaning of the word: <i lang="pt-BR">d<mark>ei</mark></i> (I gave) and <i lang="pt-BR">d<mark>ê</mark></i> (give). English speakers tend to pronounce both as a glide. Second, English speakers tend to pronounce words that are spelled with "e" with the open sound. For example, <i lang="pt-BR">probl<mark class="blue">e</mark>ma</i> with an open "e" instead of <i lang="pt-BR">probl<mark>e</mark>ma</i> with a closed "e".

The challenge for learners who speak Spanish is to recognize that not every instance of "e" is pronounced with the closed "e". They need to learn to also use the open "e". Spanish speakers do not have any difficulty saying <i lang="pt-BR">por qu<mark>ê</mark></i> but they find it more difficult to remember to say <i lang="pt-BR">at<mark class="blue">é</mark></i>.

Note: In the dialog we have shown <i lang="pt-BR">s<mark>e</mark>guro</i> and with the closed "e". However, in natural speech, this word is usually pronounced with what is called "vowel raising" where "e" is pronounced more like "i": <i lang="pt-BR">s<mark>i</mark>guro</i>. We'll deal with that in a future lesson.
