## Cultural notes

Besides the lessons in pronunciation, all of the dialogs key on some cultural issue that Valdo and Michelle have noticed as Brazilians living in the United States.

In Brazilian supermarkets there is always a person there who weighs the fruits and vegetables. In the United States it is generally the customers who have to weigh their purchases in the produce section. To our Brazilian friends, don't worry. If you don't weigh your fruit beforehand, the cashier can usually do it at the check out. To our non-Brazilian friends, get ready for some additional lines that you'll need to wait in at the supermarket.

## Pronunciation notes

In the previous lesson we looked at nasalized vowels, here we look at nasal diphthongs. These can be a little trickier. There are 5 nasalized diphthongs:

1. "<mark>ão</mark>" <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ɐ̃w]</span>, as in words like <i lang="pt-BR">s<mark>ão</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>ão</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">canç<mark>ão</mark></i>. This sound is fairly similar to the way that English speakers pronounce the second part of the word *sound* or *pound*. (Although I'm sure that linguists and language experts cringe when they hear this!) A common error is to not give these words enough nasalization, pronouncing <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>ão</mark></i> more like *pow*. This nasal diphthong is also heard in Portuguese words that end in "<mark>am</mark>", as in <i lang="pt-BR">fal<mark>am</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">cant<mark>am</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">dig<mark>am</mark></i>.
2. "<mark>ãe</mark>" <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ɐ̃j]</span>, as in words like <i lang="pt-BR">m<mark>ãe</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>ãe</mark>s</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">alem<mark>ãe</mark>s</i>. Think of repeating the English word *my, my, my, my* but give it a strong nasal quality.
3. "<mark>õe</mark>" <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[õj]</span>, as in words like <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>õe</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">canç<mark>õe</mark>s</i>. This sound is similar to what is said in the English word *coins*. This nasal diphthong is also heard among many speakers from Rio de Janeiro in words like <i lang="pt-BR">bons</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">sons</i> which are pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[bõjs]</span>, <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[sõjs]</span>.
4. "<mark>em</mark>" <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ẽj]</span>, as in the word <i lang="pt-BR">b<mark>em</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">por<mark>ém</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">tamb<mark>ém</mark></i>. This one is a little trickier because it looks like it should be just a nasal vowel and not a nasal diphthong. However, most Brazilians will pronounce these words with a strong diphthong. Words like <i lang="pt-BR">sempre</i>, especially from speakers from São Paulo, actually sound more like <i lang="pt-BR">sejmpre</i>. Their nasal diphthong is especially strong.
5. "m<mark>ui</mark>" <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ũj]</span>, as in <i lang="pt-BR">m<mark>ui</mark>to</i>. <i lang="pt-BR">M<mark>ui</mark>to</i> is pronounced with a lot of nasalization in Portuguese and so we include it here as one of the nasal diphthongs.
