## Cultural notes

Besides the lessons in pronunciation and grammar, all of the dialogs key on some cultural issue that Valdo and Michelle have noticed as Brazilians living in the United States.

"Let me see your insurance and license." The phrase that none of us wants to hear a police officer say. In Brazil car insurance is not obligatory and people usually purchase it more as a precaution against theft or accidents.

## Grammar notes

This is one case where being a speaker of Spanish really helps. The rule for making the plural of words that end in 'ão' may seem a little perplexing.

> Words that end in 'ão' are made plural by adding 'ãos', 'ões', or 'ães'.

Notice the following examples:

- <i lang="pt-BR">a mão – as m<mark>ãos</mark></i>
- <i lang="pt-BR">o cristão – os crist<mark>ãos</mark></i>
- <i lang="pt-BR">a nação – as naç<mark>ões</mark></i>
- <i lang="pt-BR">a canção – as canç<mark>ões</mark></i>
- <i lang="pt-BR">o pão – os p<mark>ães</mark></i>
- <i lang="pt-BR">o alemão – os alem<mark>ães</mark></i>

The advantage for speakers of Spanish is that they can look to the equivalents in Spanish, and then drop the consonant 'n', resulting in the Portuguese form.

| Portuguese | Spanish |
| --- | --- |
| as m<mark>ãos</mark> | las m<mark>a</mark>(n)<mark>os</mark> |
| os crist<mark>ãos</mark> | los cristi<mark>a</mark>(n)<mark>os</mark> |
| as naç<mark>ões</mark> | las naci<mark>o</mark>(n)<mark>es</mark> |
| as canç<mark>ões</mark> | las canci<mark>o</mark>(n)<mark>es</mark> |
| os <mark>pães</mark> | los p<mark>a</mark>(n)<mark>es</mark> |
| os alem<mark>ães</mark> | los alem<mark>a</mark>(n)<mark>es</mark> |

This comparison between Spanish and Portuguese does not work 100% of the time. However, it does work 95% of the time and that is enough to get you going.
