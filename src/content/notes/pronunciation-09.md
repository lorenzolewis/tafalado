## Cultural notes

Besides the lessons in pronunciation, all of the dialogs key on some cultural issue that Valdo and Michelle have noticed as Brazilians living in the United States.

For Brazilians, parking on US streets is a problem because they aren't used to the idea of paying for a limited time. In Brazil parking on streets is always free, regardless of the time that you are parked. Now, it is true that Brazil has many parking lots where one pays, but the idea of a parking meter is harder to understand.

## Pronunciation notes

In the previous lesson we have seen that Brazilian Portuguese has 7 possible vowel sounds in stressed syllables: <i lang="pt-BR">v<mark>i</mark>da</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">v<mark>e</mark>jo</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[e]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>e</mark>dra</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ɛ]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>a</mark>to</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[a]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>o</mark>de</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ɔ]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>ô</mark>de</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[o]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>u</mark>de</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[u]</span>. These 7 vowel sounds are reduced to 5 in unstressed syllables because there is no open <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ɛ]</span> and <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ɔ]</span>: <i lang="pt-BR">v<mark>i</mark>vemos</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">b<mark>e</mark>bemos</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[e]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">f<mark>a</mark>lamos</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[a]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">t<mark>o</mark>mamos</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[o]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">p<mark>u</mark>lamos</i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[u]</span>. These 5 unstressed vowel sounds are reduced to 3 in unstressed word-final position: <i lang="pt-BR">viv<mark>e</mark></i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">viv<mark>a</mark></i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ə]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">viv<mark>o</mark></i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[u]</span>. Speakers of Spanish will notice that this seems much more complicated than the 5 basic vowel sounds that Spanish maintains in all positions.

This lesson looks at a tendency that linguists call "vowel raising" (sorry for the technical jargon). It refers to times when the pronunciation of <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[o]</span> becomes <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[u]</span> and the pronunciation of <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[e]</span> becomes <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span>. We've already seen it in the case of the 3 vowel sounds in word-final position (<i lang="pt-BR">viv<mark>e</mark></i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">viv<mark>a</mark></i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[ə]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">viv<mark>o</mark></i> <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[u]</span>). However Portuguese, unlike Spanish, also has vowel raising in other instances:

1. <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[e]</span> becomes <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span> in pretonic syllables. This is a fancy way of saying that <i lang="pt-BR">b<mark>e</mark>bida</i> is pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[b<mark>i</mark>bidə]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR"><mark>e</mark>spero</i> is pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[<mark>i</mark>speru]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">pr<mark>e</mark>ciso</i> is pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[pr<mark>i</mark>sizu]</span>, etc.
2. <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[o]</span> becomes <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[u]</span> in pretonic syllables. The same process occurs with words that are spelled with "o": <i lang="pt-BR">motivo</i> is pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[m<mark>u</mark>tivu]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">moeda</i> is pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[m<mark>u</mark>edə]</span>, <i lang="pt-BR">comida</i> is pronounced <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[k<mark>u</mark>midə]</span>.

We should mention that these changes depend a lot on dialect, education, formality of speech, etc. However, the point is that, unlike Spanish where the vowel sounds remain fairly consistent no matter where they are in the word, Portuguese vowel sounds are continually changing and vowel raising is another example of that.
