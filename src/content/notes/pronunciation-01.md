## Cultural notes

Besides the lessons in pronunciation, all of the dialogs key on some cultural issue that Valdo and Michelle have noticed as Brazilians living in the United States.

In many restaurants in the US the waiter tries to leave the check on the table, as a courtesy, so that the customer can leave when ready. In Brazil waiters bring the check only when the customer asks for it, otherwise it would seem that the waiter is rushing the customers and telling them to leave.

## Pronunciation notes

This dialog focuses on the sound <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">/i/</span>. This sound is similar to what is heard in English in words such as *h<mark>ee</mark>d*, *h<mark>ea</mark>t*, *b<mark>ea</mark>d*, *b<mark>ea</mark>t*.

Excluding diphthongs and nasalization, this sound is found in two main locations:

- Any word written with the vowel "i": <i lang="pt-BR"><mark>i</mark>sso</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">s<mark>i</mark>stema</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">d<mark>i</mark>sse</i>
- Any word written with a final "e": <i lang="pt-BR">fom<mark>e</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">bif<mark>e</mark></i>, <i lang="pt-BR">troux<mark>e</mark></i>

The challenge for learners who speak English is to avoid pronouncing "i" as one would in the words *h<mark>i</mark>d*, *h<mark>i</mark>t*, *b<mark>i</mark>d*, *b<mark>i</mark>t*. Compare for example the English *v<mark>i</mark>s<mark>i</mark>t* with the Portuguese <i lang="pt-BR">v<mark>i</mark>s<mark>i</mark>ta</i>.

The challenge for learners who speak Spanish is to avoid pronouncing the word-final "e" as one would in Spanish <i lang="es">b<mark>e</mark>b<mark>e</mark></i>, <i lang="es">dic<mark>e</mark></i>, <i lang="es">com<mark>e</mark></i>. Compare for example the Spanish <i lang="es">v<mark>i</mark>v<mark>e</mark></i> with the Portuguese <i lang="pt-BR">v<mark>i</mark>v<mark>e</mark></i>.
