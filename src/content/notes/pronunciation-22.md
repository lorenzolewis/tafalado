## Cultural notes

Besides the lessons in pronunciation, all of the dialogs key on some cultural issue that Valdo and Michelle have noticed as Brazilians living in the United States.

Fast food, what a blessing! What a curse! It's easy for Valdo and Michelle to get caught up in the fast pace of life, especially as university students. The temptation is to do the same with their meals. It may be that Americans like to eat on the run, but Brazilians prefer to sit down and have a real meal, especially during the afternoon. From professional executives to blue collar workers, all take time out at lunch to eat more than just a quick sandwich. Once you get into the habit of rice and beans for lunch, why go with a little hot dog?

## Pronunciation notes

Obstruent clusters and epenthetic vowels. Unless you have studied linguistics, it is hard to believe that anyone would understand those words. It is one of the features, however, that gives Brazilian Portuguese a really cool sound. OK, first off, Brazilian words do not generally end with a consonant. So, when words that are borrowed from English are adopted into Portuguese, a vowel is often added to the end of the word. That is why it is fun to hear words like *ping pong* (<i lang="pt-BR">pingue pongue</i>), *King Kong* (<i lang="pt-BR">kingue kongue</i>), *picnic* (<i lang="pt-BR">piquenique</i>), and Orlando's personal favorite *vicks vapor rub* (<i lang="pt-BR">viquevaporube</i>). Similarly in the dialog, Valdo and Michelle say fast food (fasti foodi) and hot dog (hoti doggie) with vowel sounds at the end. And in the case of those words, we also hear the palatalized versions of <i lang="pt-BR">ti</i> and <i lang="pt-BR">di</i>.

Unlike <i lang="pt-BR"><mark>pr</mark></i> in <i lang="pt-BR"><mark>pr</mark>ato</i> and <i lang="pt-BR"><mark>dr</mark></i> in <i lang="pt-BR"><mark>dr</mark>ama</i>, which have consonant clusters in the same syllable, obstruent clusters refer to words that have consonants that are grouped together in different syllables: <i lang="pt-BR">ri<mark class="blue">t</mark>.<mark>m</mark>o</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">a<mark class="blue">d</mark>.<mark>v</mark>ogado</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">a<mark class="blue">b</mark>.<mark>s</mark>urdo</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">di<mark class="blue">g</mark>.<mark>n</mark>o</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">o<mark class="blue">b</mark>.<mark>t</mark>er</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">su<mark class="blue">b</mark>.<mark>s</mark>tituir</i>. The tendency in Portuguese is to "break up" these clusters by inserting the vowel <span lang="pt-BR-fonipa">[i]</span> in between the two consonants. The result is something like: <i lang="pt-BR">ri<mark class="blue">ti</mark>.<mark>m</mark>o</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">a<mark class="blue">di</mark>.<mark>v</mark>ogado</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">a<mark class="blue">bi</mark>.<mark>s</mark>urdo</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">di<mark class="blue">gi</mark>.<mark>n</mark>o</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">o<mark class="blue">bi</mark>.<mark>t</mark>er</i>, <i lang="pt-BR">su<mark class="blue">bi</mark>.<mark>s</mark>tituir</i>. Additionally, as soon as a word has a <i lang="pt-BR">ti</i> or <i lang="pt-BR">di</i>, the regular palatalization also follows, as in the case of <i lang="pt-BR">ritmo</i> and <i lang="pt-BR">advogado</i> above.

In Spanish we also see that these obstruent clusters tend to change in natural speech. The difference is that instead of adding an extra sound, Spanish speakers tend to reduce or eliminate sounds. For example, even though the word for *rhythm* and *worthy* are spelled the same in both languages, <i lang="es">ritmo</i> and <i lang="es">digno</i>, sound extremely different.

This tendency is so strong that we have even met Brazilians who thought that the English word for *walkie talkie* was *walktalk*. Why? Because if <i lang="pt-BR">pingue pongue</i> is *ping pong* and <i lang="pt-BR">piquenique</i> is *picnic*, by analogy, *walkie talkie* would be a *walktalk*. Why not?
